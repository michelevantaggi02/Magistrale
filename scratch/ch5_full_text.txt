ML algorithms for classification aim to optimize models that can estimate categorical

for object recognition allows to classify the content of an image with respect to the
category of object it depicts (e.g., chair, table, person, bottle, ...). In practice, regardless

numerical form, specifically as integers. It is important to note that, although numerical
encoding naturally creates an order between classes (e.g., ”Chair” → 1, ”Person” → 2,
”Bottle” → 3), this should not be understood as such. The fact that t ∈ {1, 2, 3, . . . , C}
only indicates that the output can take one value among the possible C.

Classification Approaches

In the previous chapter, we studied K-NN classifier and Logistic regression as possible
solutions to classification problems. We now take a step back to study the more general
categories of classification approaches. In general, three basic families of approaches can
be distinguished, each of which may be more or less suitable depending on our design
needs. The choice of one category of approaches over another is related to the interest
we have in modeling certain process features, in the amount of data available and in the
information needed to train the models.
To study the characteristics of these families of approaches, we more formally define
the concept of decision function. In particular, the decision function is a function
f (·) : RD → St which, given an input feature vector x, provides as output the class
estimate associated with x, i.e., t̂ = f (x), with t̂ ∈ St = {1, . . . , K}. In some situations,
the decision function is sufficient for us. However, sometimes we may be interested not
only in the predicted class, but also in the probability of that prediction, i.e. p(t|x).
Finally, in some situations it is important to model not only the probability of the class
given the input, but also the joint probability p(t, x). In general, if we are interested
in estimating the statistical properties of the unknown distribution of interest from the
data, we aim to perform Inference, and, with respect to what we want to infer, three
families of approaches can be distinguished:
• Discriminant Function: This family of approaches make use of discriminant
functions. They do not aim to infer the statistical characteristics of the unknown
distribution, but merely identify a decision function f (x) directly capable of predicting the category associated with the input. The decision function is clearly
designed with respect to optimality criteria (loss functions).
• Discriminative Model: Discriminative model approaches instead attempt to
make inference about the a posteriori probability distribution p(t|x), i.e., the statistical process that describes how labels are generated from features. Logistic
regression and K-NN classifier are two approaches that belong to this category.
In the case of Logistic Regression, we modeled p(t|x) as:
p(t|x) = σ(wT x)t (1 − σ(wT x))1−t

(5.37)

where σ(wT x) = g(wT x) (we used σ to make the sigmoid function explicit). Thus,
in Logistic regression, once we have determined the optimal parameter vector, we
are able to obtain an estimate of the probability of the output via the Bernoulli distribution. Furthermore, once we have estimated p(t|x), we can obtain the decision
function by simply finding a threshold with which to compare the class probability,
e.g.:

5. Classification Algorithms

82

p(t = 1|x) = σ(wT x) ≥ 0.5

(5.38)

N.B. In this case, having available the posterior distribution of the classes, the
decision function is easily modifiable simply choosing a new threshold. Instead,
in the case of the discriminant functions, to change the decision function we need
to perform the training from scratch.
• Generative Model: Instead, these approaches aim at inferring the joint distribution p(t, x) by estimating the class-conditional distribution p(x|t) and the
prior over classes p(t). Once the joint distribution is modeled, it is possible to
obtain the posterior with Bayes’ theorem and, consequently, determine a decision
function:
p(t|x) =

p(x|t)p(t)
∝ p(x|t)p(t)
p(x)

(5.39)

Clearly, in this case, we have to make a higher modeling effort, since we have to
model the data generation process from the joint distribution. In particular, since
p(t, x) = p(x|t)p(t), in this case we need to model both p(x|t) and p(t).
Modeling the joint distribution means having the generation model of the data
available. Consequently, this family of approaches is the most general possible and
includes the other two categories. In addition, generative models make additional
assumptions about the process that generates the features x given the classes and
about the prior distribution over the classes. They are, thus, closer to Bayesian
formulations and, in general, are more robust to overfitting.

5.7

Quick Detour: Classification Metrics

As with the loss function, the metrics introduced for regression problems are not adequate
for classification problems and we must therefore introduce new ones.
If we consider the case of binary classification, the output of the algorithm can be
categorized with respect to 4 possible scenarios depending on the correctness of the
classification. If we consider class ”1” as positive and class ”0” as negative, we can
define the following 4 scenarios:
• True Positive TP: In the case where the class estimated by the algorithm is the
positive one and the sample is associated with a positive label (”1”);
• True Negative TN: In the case where the class estimated by the algorithm is
the negative class and the sample is associated with a negative label (”0”);
• False Positive FP: In the case where the estimated class is positive, but the
sample is actually associated with a negative label;

• False Negative FN: in the case where the estimated class is negative, but the
sample is actually associated with a positive label.

samples that are actually positive. Conversely, if we lower the decision threshold to
increase recall, we will have more incorrect positive classifications.

The P-R curve allows to choose the optimal threshold value with respect to the

to make a mistake in predicting the positive class or if it is more important to ”find” as
many positive samples as possible.

is the one with fewer samples. On Balanced datasets, on the other hand, it is more
appropriate to use the AUC of the ROC Curve.
