to make a mistake in predicting the positive class or if it is more important to ”find” as

6.2.1

Sufficient Statistic

Definition 6.1. Sufficient Statistic: Consider a set X0 , X1 , . . . , XN −1 of random
samples obtained from a distribution characterized by an unknown parameter w. Then
the statistic:
T = u(X0 , X1 , . . . , XN −1 )

(6.8)

is called sufficient for w if p(X0 , X1 , . . . , XN −1 |T )⊥w.
This means that if the conditional distribution of the samples X0 , X1 , . . . , XN −1 given
the statistic T does not depend on w then T is sufficient. In other words, T contains all
the information needed to estimate the parameter w, i.e., T is a sufficient ”summary”
of the data for the purpose of inference about w. If T is sufficient, in fact we have
p(w|X0 , X1 , . . . , XN −1 , T ) = p(w|T ).
A sufficient statistic is therefore an excellent tool to ”condense” the data without
losing information for the subsequent inference step through, for example, MLE. However, following the definition, to figure out if a statistic is sufficient we need to compute
the conditional distribution of X0 , X1 , . . . , XN −1 given T , which in practical cases, may
not be a viable route. An easier alternative to figure out if a statistic is sufficient is to
use the Factorization Theorem:

Let us now see what are some possible structural choices for the class-conditional
distributions p(x|Ck ) and for the prior p(Ck ). The choices we make with respect to
these distributions are crucial, since they lead to different generative models.

7.1

Gaussian Class Conditional Densities

distribution is the Gaussian. The family of models obtained with this choice are referred to as Gaussian Discriminant Analysis (GDA) models. In the following, we
will introduce two of them: Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) and Quadratic
Discriminant Analysis (QDA).
